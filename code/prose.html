In the Cheviots or the Welsh Marches, almost every hillfort has another in view. That has fed an old idea: that hillforts were placed to see each other, perhaps to pass signals by fire or smoke along a chain of hilltops. The trouble with the idea is that hills see hills. If you put forts on high ground, they will see each other whether anyone planned it or not.

So I tested it. I took all 4,147 hillforts in the Atlas of Hillforts of Britain and Ireland and traced a line of sight across the ground between every pair less than 30 km apart: {{network.30.pairs_in_range|int}} lines in all. Then I moved every fort to a random spot 1 to 5 km away on similar ground and did it again, twenty times over. If the real forts see each other more than the shifted ones do, siting was doing something that the hills alone do not.

The short answer: they do, by about {{null.20.excess_pct|pct0}}. But the longer answer is more interesting. Most or all of that extra comes from each fort sitting in a spot with a wider view than its surroundings, not from forts being aimed at one another.

    03 · ChainsA message from Flintshire to Devon

    If fort A can see fort B, and B can see C, a signal could in principle pass from A to C. Joining up every visible pair turns the hillforts into networks. Within 20 km, the largest has {{network.20.largest_component|int}} forts and spreads across {{network.20.top_components.0.span_km|f1}} km of the Scottish Borders, Northumberland, the Lothians and beyond. The second largest, with {{network.20.top_components.1.size|int}} forts, runs from the Welsh borders down to Wessex and the south-west.

    That second network gives the longest relay I found. A signal passed fort to fort could in principle travel from {{network.20.farthest_relay.start_short|raw}}, to {{network.20.farthest_relay.end_short|raw}}, {{network.20.farthest_relay.straight_km|int}} km apart in a straight line, in {{network.20.farthest_relay.hops|int}} hops. Within the largest network, the route that needs the most hops between any two forts takes {{network.20.diameter.hops|int}}.

    At the other end, {{network.20.component_size_bins.1|int}} forts stand alone, and many more sit in small groups of two to ten. Allow 30 km and the largest network grows to {{network.30.largest_component|int}} forts; cut it to 10 km and it shrinks to {{network.10.largest_component|int}}.

    A chain on a map is not a chain in the past. Nobody knows which of these forts were in use at the same time.

    Hillforts were built and used over more than a thousand years, mostly in the late Bronze Age and Iron Age, with some reused much later. A relay of {{network.20.farthest_relay.hops|int}} hops assumes every fort along it was standing and in use at once. The networks here are what the ground allows, not what people did.

    04 · The testDo they see more than hills do?

    To test the signalling idea, I needed a fair comparison. For each fort I drew 500 random points between 1 and 5 km away and kept the ones on similar ground: about as high, and holding a similar rank in height within 2 km (a fort on the highest ground around is matched to points that are also on the highest ground around). {{null_matching.share_forts_matched|pct0}} of forts had at least one match. I then built twenty shifted maps, each with every fort moved to one of its matches, and traced the sight lines again.

    The shifted maps keep the regional pattern, the density of forts and the kind of ground they sit on. What they lose is the exact spot each fort was built.

    So the siting was not random with respect to view. But that does not yet mean forts were placed to see each other. There is a simpler explanation: each fort was placed somewhere with a good all-round view, and two places with good views are more likely to see each other.

    Seeing far, or seeing each other?

    To split the two, I ran a mixed test: a real fort looking at the shifted position of its neighbour. If forts were simply built in more open spots, a real fort should see its neighbour's shifted position more often than two shifted points see each other, and the gain should roughly double when both ends are real. If forts were aimed at each other, two real forts should do much better than that.

    A real fort looking at a shifted neighbour within 20 km gets a line of sight {{null.20.cross_share_mean|pct1}} of the time, against {{null.20.null_share_mean|pct1}} for two shifted points. Apply that gain at both ends and you would expect {{null.20.expected_if_sites_only|pct1}} of real pairs to be in sight. The real figure is {{null.20.observed_share|pct1}}, almost exactly what good views alone predict. Within 10 km the real figure is a little higher than predicted ({{null.10.observed_share|pct1}} against {{null.10.expected_if_sites_only|pct1}}); within 30 km it is lower ({{null.30.observed_share|pct1}} against {{null.30.expected_if_sites_only|pct1}}).

    The extra intervisibility looks like a by-product of good views, not of forts aimed at each other.

    In other words, almost all of the extra intervisibility can be explained by each fort having a wider view than the ground around it. At most there is a small extra between close neighbours: about {{null.10.pair_excess_pct|pct0}} more pairs in sight within 10 km than good views alone predict. I find no sign, at this national scale, of forts being placed to see distant neighbours in particular. That does not rule out signalling in particular places or periods. It does mean the national pattern needs no signalling to explain it.

    The big networks are not special either. Within 20 km the largest real network has {{null.20.observed_largest|int}} forts; the largest networks on the shifted maps have {{null.20.null_largest_min|int}} to {{null.20.null_largest_max|int}}. Chains that long are what you get from this many forts on this kind of ground.

    05 · Who sees moreSize helps a little; the coast does not

    Raw counts favour multivallate forts, with two or more ramparts. They see {{attributes.groups.multivallate.mean_visible|f1}} other forts on average within 20 km, against {{attributes.groups.univallate.mean_visible|f1}} for forts with a single rampart. But multivallate forts also have more neighbours: {{attributes.groups.multivallate.mean_in_range|f1}} within 20 km on average, against {{attributes.groups.univallate.mean_in_range|f1}}. Count only the share of neighbours a fort can see, and allow for size, height and nation, and the rampart difference disappears.

    The chart shows that model. Each row is the change in the odds that a fort can see a given neighbour within 20 km. The strongest factor by far is how high the fort sits relative to the ground within 2 km. Bigger forts see slightly more: each doubling of the enclosed area multiplies the odds by about {{attributes.glm_share_visible_20km.rows.0.odds_ratio|f2}}. Coastal forts see far fewer: the odds are {{attributes.glm_share_visible_20km.rows.2.odds_ratio|f2}} times those of inland forts.     07 · LimitsWhat a line of sight is not

    Seeing a line is not seeing a fort. A line of sight means nothing solid is in the way. Whether a person could make out a rampart 15 km away depends on light, haze and what they were looking for. A fire at night or a column of smoke by day would carry much further than the outline of a bank.

    The ground model is coarse. The elevation data have pixels about 90 m across and smooth off summits and crags. Trees, hedges and buildings are not in it, and nobody knows the Iron Age tree cover hill by hill.

    Fort points are not summits. The Atlas gives one point per fort, which is not necessarily the highest point or the rampart. On a broad hilltop, the edge of a fort can have a better view than the recorded point. Some well-known pairs fail for this reason. British Camp and Midsummer Hill in the Malverns, {{sanity_pairs.0.km|f1}} km apart, are blocked from their recorded points but visible once both are moved to the highest ground inside an area the size of each fort. Hambledon Hill and Hod Hill in Dorset fail either way in this model. Moving each fort to its highest ground raises the share of pairs in sight within 20 km from {{network.20.share_visible|pct1}} to {{network.20.share_visible_snapped|pct1}}, and a 5 m watcher (a person on a rampart or a fire on a platform) raises it to {{network.20.share_visible_5m|pct1}}.

    Dates. The Atlas holds forts from different centuries. The networks show what the ground allows, not a system anyone used.

    How I did this

    Sites. All 4,147 hillforts in the Atlas of Hillforts of Britain and Ireland (Lock and Ralston 2017), via the Atlas source data released with Mike Middleton's Hillforts Primer, as used in my companion study The Famous Few.

    Ground. AWS Terrain Tiles (Mapzen/Tilezen) at zoom 10, about 90 m pixels at these latitudes, sampled with bilinear interpolation. Sea is 0 m.

    Line of sight. For every pair of forts up to 30 km apart, I sampled the ground every 45 m along the straight line between them. Eye and target are 2 m above the ground at each fort's recorded point. A sample blocks the line if the ground, raised by the bulge of the earth between the two forts (allowing for standard refraction, k = 0.13), reaches the line. I checked the code on synthetic ground: flat ground stays visible up to the expected horizon, and a ridge blocks the view when it should. I also ran the analysis with 5 m eyes and with each fort moved to the highest ground within a circle of its recorded area (45 to 300 m radius).

    Networks. Forts are linked when they are in line of sight. Networks are the connected groups of linked forts. Relay routes use the fewest hops.

    Null model. For each fort, 500 random points 1 to 5 km away, kept if on land, within 0.05 of the fort's height rank within 2 km, and within 50 m or 25% of its elevation. Twenty shifted maps each move every fort to one of its matches (to a random one of its matches for the {{null_matching.share_forts_matched|pct1}} of forts with at least one, and to one of its five closest candidates for the rest). Shifted forts average {{null_matching.null_mean_elev|f1}} m above sea level against {{null_matching.fort_mean_elev|f1}} m for the real ones. The mixed test pairs each real fort with the shifted positions of its neighbours, in both directions.

    Models. A binomial model of visible neighbours out of neighbours within 20 km, with robust standard errors and nation controls. All numbers on this page come from the published results file.
